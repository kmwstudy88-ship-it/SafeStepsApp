LESSON 1.1: WHAT IS THE NERVOUS SYSTEM AND WHY DOES IT MATTER?
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can describe the nervous system in their own words and explain why it is relevant to their parenting.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
The nervous system is your body&apos;s communication network — it controls everything from breathing to how you react under stress
It developed over millions of years to keep us safe from danger
In people who have experienced trauma, the nervous system can become over-sensitive — like a smoke alarm that goes off when making toast
This is not a character flaw — it is biology shaped by experience
Understanding your nervous system is the first step to understanding your reactions as a parent
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Draw a simple outline of a body and mark where you feel stress — head, chest, stomach, hands
In pairs, share one situation where your body reacted before your brain caught up
Watch a short video on the stress response and discuss as a group
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
When did you first notice your body reacting strongly to stress?
What does your body do when you feel under threat?
How might your nervous system reactions affect your children?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write a short paragraph in your own words explaining what the nervous system is and why understanding it matters for your parenting.
LESSON 1.2: YOUR BRAIN IN THREE PARTS — SURVIVAL, EMOTIONAL, THINKING
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify the three brain regions and understand how trauma affects which part is in control.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
The brain can be understood in three layers — the survival brain (brainstem), the emotional brain (limbic), and the thinking brain (prefrontal cortex)
The survival brain controls basic functions and threat responses — it is always on
The emotional brain processes feelings and memories — especially trauma memories
The thinking brain handles reasoning, planning, empathy and decision-making — but it goes offline under threat
Trauma causes the survival and emotional brain to take over, making the thinking brain less accessible
This is why parents under stress sometimes react in ways they later regret — their thinking brain was not available
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Make a model of the three-part brain using your hand — palm is survival, closed fist is emotional, fingers folded over is thinking brain
Discuss: What happens to your thinking brain when you are really scared or angry?
Practise &apos;flipping the lid&apos; — show how the thinking brain goes offline and comes back
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Which part of your brain do you feel most driven by when things are hard?
Can you think of a time your thinking brain went offline? What happened?
What helps your thinking brain come back online?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Describe the three parts of the brain in your own words and give one example of how each one shows up in your parenting.
LESSON 1.3: FIGHT, FLIGHT, FREEZE AND FAWN — RECOGNISING YOUR RESPONSES
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify their primary trauma response and understand it as a survival strategy.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
When the brain detects danger it activates one of four survival responses — fight, flight, freeze or fawn
Fight: becoming aggressive, angry, confrontational
Flight: running away, avoiding, leaving situations
Freeze: shutting down, going blank, becoming very still
Fawn: people-pleasing, agreeing with everything to keep the peace
These responses developed to protect us — they were survival strategies that worked in dangerous situations
In parenting, these responses can be triggered by ordinary stress — a crying child, a bill, a confrontation with a partner
Understanding your response helps you choose a different one
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Self-assessment quiz: Which response do you most commonly use?
Role play common parenting scenarios and identify which response gets activated
Group discussion: How did your primary response help you survive your childhood?
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Which response do you most recognise in yourself?
When does it get activated as a parent?
How does your response affect your children when it comes out?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write about your primary trauma response — what it looks like, when it shows up, and how it affects your parenting. Include one way you could respond differently.
LESSON 1.4: WHAT TRIGGERS MEAN AND WHERE THEY COME FROM
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify their personal triggers and trace them to their origin.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
A trigger is when something in the present activates a strong emotional or physical response rooted in a past experience
Triggers are not random — they are linked to memories, experiences and learned associations
Common parenting triggers include a child crying, feeling ignored, being criticised, mess, noise, feeling out of control
Triggers often feel disproportionate to the current situation because the reaction belongs to the past not the present
Identifying triggers gives you the power to prepare and respond differently
Triggers are not an excuse for harmful behaviour — but understanding them is essential for change
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Complete a personal trigger map — list your top five triggers and what past experience they may be linked to
Rate each trigger on a 1-10 intensity scale
Identify what physical sensations accompany each trigger
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What are your most powerful triggers as a parent?
Where do you think they come from?
What happens in your body just before you are triggered?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Create a personal trigger map identifying at least five triggers, their physical sensations, and their likely origin. Include one coping strategy for each.
LESSON 1.5: HOW YOUR NERVOUS SYSTEM AFFECTS YOUR CHILDREN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand that their nervous system state directly impacts their children&apos;s nervous system and development.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Children&apos;s nervous systems are immature and depend on their parent&apos;s nervous system to regulate
When a parent is calm, their child&apos;s nervous system can settle — this is called co-regulation
When a parent is dysregulated, their child&apos;s nervous system activates in response — even if the parent says nothing
Children are extraordinarily sensitive to parental stress — they read body language, tone and energy before words
Chronic exposure to a dysregulated parent creates chronic stress in a child&apos;s nervous system
The good news — a parent who learns to regulate themselves is giving their child one of the greatest gifts possible
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Watch a video clip of a parent and child interacting — identify nervous system states in both
Experiment with tone of voice — same words, different energy — and observe how it lands differently
Discuss: What did your parent&apos;s nervous system feel like to be around as a child?
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
How do your children respond when you are stressed?
Can you recall a time your dysregulation affected your child?
What do you notice in your children when you are calm?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write a reflection on how your nervous system state affects each of your children specifically. Include two commitments you are making to regulate yourself for their benefit.
LESSON 1.6: WINDOW OF TOLERANCE — FINDING YOUR CALM ZONE
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can explain the window of tolerance and identify what keeps them in or pushes them out of it.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
The Window of Tolerance is the zone in which we can function well — we can think, feel, connect and parent effectively
Above the window is hyperarousal — panic, rage, overwhelm, hypervigilance
Below the window is hypoarousal — shutdown, numbness, disconnection, depression
Trauma narrows the window — small things push us out
Healing widens the window — we can handle more before being pushed out
The goal is not to never leave the window — it is to notice when you have left and return more quickly
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Draw your personal window of tolerance — mark what pushes you up, what pushes you down, and what keeps you in
Rate your window size today on a 1-10 scale
Identify three things that reliably bring you back into your window
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What does it feel like to be in your window of tolerance?
What are the early signs that you are leaving it?
What helps you return?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Create a personalised Window of Tolerance diagram with at least five triggers above and below the window, and five strategies for returning to the window.
LESSON 1.7: WHAT DYSREGULATION LOOKS AND FEELS LIKE IN YOUR BODY
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify their unique physical signs of dysregulation before they reach a crisis point.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Dysregulation is when your nervous system moves out of the window of tolerance
Physical signs of hyperarousal dysregulation: heart racing, jaw clenching, shallow breathing, feeling hot, tunnel vision, shaking
Physical signs of hypoarousal dysregulation: heaviness, going blank, feeling numb, slowed thinking, wanting to sleep or disappear
Everyone&apos;s dysregulation has a unique physical signature — learning yours is critical
The earlier you notice your signs, the more options you have
Most parents in the program have lived with dysregulation so long it feels normal — recalibrating takes time
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Body scan exercise — sit quietly and notice physical sensations from head to toe
Create a personal dysregulation profile — your specific physical signs for both hyper and hypo states
Pair share: describe what your body feels like just before you lose it
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What does dysregulation feel like in your body?
How early do you usually notice it?
What have the consequences been when you missed the signs?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write a detailed personal dysregulation profile including your physical signs of both hyperarousal and hypoarousal, and identify at which point you need to intervene to prevent escalation.
LESSON 1.8: EARLY WARNING SIGNS — NOTICING BEFORE YOU REACT
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify their personal early warning signs and use them as a signal to act before escalation.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Early warning signs are the first subtle indicators that your nervous system is becoming activated
They appear well before dysregulation — if you catch them here you have the most options
Common early signs: irritability, shortened responses, physical tension, urge to escape, overthinking
Everyone&apos;s early signs are unique — yours may be different
Building the habit of noticing early signs is one of the most powerful parenting skills you can develop
Using early signs as a signal to use a strategy — not as evidence that something is wrong with you
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Create an Early Warning Signs card — your top five signs in order of appearance
Practise noticing early signs during a group discussion about a mildly stressful topic
Share with a trusted person in the group — ask them what early signs they notice in you
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What are your earliest signs that stress is building?
How long do you usually have between the first sign and losing control?
What has helped you catch it early in the past?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Develop a personal Early Warning Signs Action Plan — list your signs in order, and identify one action you will take at each stage to prevent escalation.
LESSON 1.9: GROUNDING TECHNIQUES — BRINGING YOURSELF BACK
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can use at least three grounding techniques effectively to return to the window of tolerance.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Grounding brings you back to the present moment when you are being pulled into past trauma reactions
Grounding works by activating the senses — smell, touch, sight, sound, taste — which bypass the trauma response
The 5-4-3-2-1 technique: 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste
Cold water on the face or wrists activates the dive reflex and slows the heart rate
Feet firmly on the floor — noticing the ground beneath you
Holding something textured and describing it in detail
Grounding does not make problems go away — it makes you safe enough to deal with them
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Practise 5-4-3-2-1 as a group — notice what changes in your body
Experiment with cold water on wrists — rate your stress before and after
Create a personal grounding kit — five items that help ground you
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Which grounding technique works best for you?
When could you use grounding in your daily parenting life?
How could you teach your children to use grounding?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Practise and document three grounding techniques over one week. Record when you used them, what triggered the need, and how effective they were on a 1-10 scale.
LESSON 1.10: BREATHING AND BODY-BASED REGULATION TOOLS
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can use breathing and body-based techniques to regulate their nervous system in real time.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Breathing is the only automatic function of the nervous system we can consciously control — making it a direct route to regulation
Box breathing: inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4 — activates the parasympathetic system
Extended exhale breathing: make the exhale longer than the inhale — directly calms the nervous system
Progressive muscle relaxation — tensing and releasing muscle groups
Humming, singing and vocalisation — vibrates the vagus nerve
Movement — shaking, walking, dancing to discharge stress hormones
These tools work biologically — they are not just &apos;calming down&apos; advice
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Practice box breathing together as a group — 5 rounds
Try extended exhale breathing — notice the difference
Experiment with humming for 60 seconds and notice what changes
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Which breathing technique felt most accessible to you?
When in your day could you practise these regularly?
How could you use these with your children?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Commit to practising one breathing technique daily for two weeks. Keep a log recording when you practised, what was happening, and how it affected you.
LESSON 1.11: CO-REGULATION — HOW CALM PARENTS CALM CHILDREN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand co-regulation and can describe how their regulated state directly benefits their child.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Co-regulation is when one person&apos;s regulated nervous system helps another person regulate
It is the biological mechanism behind why a calm parent soothes a distressed child
Babies cannot self-regulate at all — they are entirely dependent on caregiver co-regulation
Young children can begin to self-regulate only after thousands of experiences of co-regulation from safe adults
Co-regulation is not spoiling or giving in — it is neurological scaffolding
When you regulate yourself first, you are not abandoning your child — you are preparing to help them
The oxygen mask principle — you must regulate yourself to regulate your child
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Role play a child having a tantrum — practise staying regulated as the adult
Video observation — identify co-regulation moments between parents and children
Reflect on a time someone&apos;s calm presence helped you regulate
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Can you recall someone who co-regulated you as a child? What did they do?
What does your child need from you when they are distressed?
What gets in the way of you co-regulating your child?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Observe and document three co-regulation moments with your child over one week. Describe what happened, how you stayed regulated, and how your child responded.
LESSON 1.12: BUILDING A PERSONAL REGULATION PLAN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents create a comprehensive personal regulation plan they can use in daily parenting life.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
A regulation plan brings together everything learned in this course into a practical daily tool
It includes: your triggers, your early warning signs, your grounding tools, your breathing techniques, your co-regulation strategies
It is personalised — what works for you may not work for someone else
It should be visible and accessible — not just in a folder
Family members and workers can know your plan — it is not a secret
A regulation plan is a living document — it grows and changes as you do
Having a plan reduces shame — you are not just reacting, you are responding with intention
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Complete the Regulation Plan template — all sections
Share one section of your plan with the group
Identify who in your life can support your plan
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What part of this course has been most valuable to you?
What will you do differently as a parent as a result of this course?
What support do you need to implement your plan?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Submit your completed Personal Regulation Plan. It must include: top five triggers, early warning signs in order, three grounding techniques, two breathing tools, co-regulation strategy for each child, and one commitment statement.

LESSON 2.1: WHAT IS INTERGENERATIONAL TRAUMA?
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can define intergenerational trauma and give examples of how it is passed between generations.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Intergenerational trauma is when the effects of trauma are passed from one generation to the next
It can be passed through parenting behaviours, attachment patterns, nervous system responses, and even epigenetically
If your parents experienced trauma and it was not addressed, it shaped how they parented you
How they parented you shaped your nervous system, beliefs and behaviours
Without intervention, you may parent in ways that reflect their trauma — even if your intentions are different
The cycle is powerful but it is not destiny — awareness and support can break it
Many parents in the child protection system are themselves survivors of childhood harm
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Group discussion: What did you learn about parenting from watching your own parents?
Complete the sentence: &apos;In my family, it was normal to...&apos;
Identify one thing you want to do the same as your parents, and one thing you want to do differently
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What patterns do you notice repeating in your family across generations?
What did you learn about love, safety and discipline from your childhood?
What kind of parent did you always want to be?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write a one-page reflection on what intergenerational trauma means to you personally and how you believe it has shown up in your own life.
LESSON 2.2: YOUR ACE SCORE — WHAT HAPPENED TO YOU MATTERS
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents complete an ACE assessment, understand their score, and recognise the impact of adverse childhood experiences.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
ACE stands for Adverse Childhood Experiences — the landmark study that changed how we understand childhood trauma
The original ACE study found that childhood experiences of abuse, neglect and household dysfunction have profound lifelong effects
The 10 ACE categories: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect, domestic violence, substance use in household, mental illness in household, incarceration of household member, parental separation
The higher the ACE score, the greater the risk of health, mental health and relationship difficulties in adulthood
ACE scores explain history — they do not determine destiny
Understanding your ACE score builds empathy for yourself and helps explain — not excuse — your behaviours as a parent
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Complete the ACE questionnaire privately
Small group discussion about the ACE study findings — without sharing individual scores unless chosen
Explore the &apos;What if&apos; question: What if the adults in your childhood had gotten help?
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
How do you feel having completed the ACE questionnaire?
What do you wish the adults in your life had known about the impact of your childhood experiences?
How does understanding your ACE score change how you see yourself?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Write a reflection on your ACE score — what it represents, how your childhood experiences have affected you, and what you want to be different for your children.
LESSON 2.3: HOW CHILDHOOD EXPERIENCES SHAPE OUR BELIEFS ABOUT PARENTING
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify specific beliefs about parenting they formed in childhood and examine whether they still serve them.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Children learn what parenting looks like by watching and experiencing it
These observations become core beliefs about what parents do, how they show love, how conflict is handled, what children deserve
Common harmful inherited beliefs: children should be seen and not heard, toughening children up prepares them for the world, showing emotion is weakness, physical punishment is discipline, love means never saying sorry
These beliefs feel true and normal because they were the water you swam in as a child
Examining and questioning these beliefs is not disrespecting your parents — it is choosing what to keep and what to leave
New beliefs can be consciously chosen and practised until they become natural
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Complete the &apos;Inherited Beliefs&apos; worksheet — list beliefs about parenting, discipline, emotion and love learned in childhood
Rate each belief: does this still serve my children? Yes/No/Unsure
Choose three beliefs to actively replace and identify what you want to believe instead
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
Which childhood belief about parenting is hardest to let go of?
What belief are you most proud to be passing on?
What does a child need that your childhood beliefs did not provide?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Complete the Inherited Beliefs worksheet and write a paragraph for each of three beliefs you are consciously choosing to change, explaining why and what you are replacing them with.
LESSON 2.4: MAPPING YOUR FAMILY PATTERNS ACROSS GENERATIONS
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents create a three-generation genogram identifying patterns of trauma, strength and change.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
A genogram is like a family tree but includes patterns of behaviour, relationships and experiences
Mapping three generations reveals patterns that might otherwise be invisible
Common patterns that appear across generations: addiction, domestic violence, mental illness, early parenthood, child removal, incarceration
Patterns also include strengths: resilience, creativity, cultural connection, faith, community
Seeing the pattern across generations depersonalises it — it is not just you, and it is not inevitable
Your generation can be the one that changes the pattern — with support
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Create a three-generation genogram with worker support
Identify patterns that repeat across generations — mark in red
Identify strengths that appear across generations — mark in green
Identify where someone in the family tried to break a pattern — mark in blue
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS
What surprised you when you saw your family mapped out?
Which pattern are you most determined to break?
Which strength do you want to carry forward?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Submit your completed three-generation genogram with a written reflection identifying three patterns you are committed to changing and three strengths you are building on.
LESSON 2.5: THE PARENTING YOU RECEIVED VS THE PARENTING YOU WANT TO GIVE
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can clearly articulate the difference between the parenting they received and the parenting they want to provide.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Many parents in this program did not receive the parenting they needed as children

LESSON 3.1: THE FIRST 2000 DAYS — WHY THEY MATTER MOST
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can explain why the first 2000 days of life are critical for brain development.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
The first 2000 days — from conception to approximately age five — are the most critical period of brain development in a human lifetime
More than one million new neural connections are made every second in the first years of life
The experiences a child has in these years physically shape the architecture of their brain
Positive experiences — warmth, safety, responsiveness, play — build strong neural pathways
Negative experiences — fear, neglect, stress, inconsistency — also shape the brain, but in ways that create challenges
The brain is more plastic (able to change) in early childhood than at any other time
This is not about blame — it is about opportunity. The brain can heal with the right support

LESSON 3.2: HOW A BABY&apos;S BRAIN DEVELOPS FROM BIRTH
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand newborn brain development and know what a baby needs from them in the earliest months.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
A newborn&apos;s brain is approximately 25% of its adult size — it grows to 80% by age three

Every responsive interaction a parent has with a baby is literally building their brain

Babies cannot be spoiled — their needs are neurological, not manipulative

LESSON 3.4: HOW STRESS AND FEAR AFFECT A DEVELOPING BRAIN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand the neurological impact of stress and fear on a child&apos;s developing brain.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
When a child experiences stress or fear, the brain releases cortisol — the stress hormone
Short-term, manageable stress followed by comfort is normal and even builds resilience
But chronic, severe or unpredictable stress — called toxic stress — physically damages the developing brain
Toxic stress keeps the survival brain permanently activated, preventing the higher brain from developing properly
Effects of toxic stress: difficulty learning, emotional dysregulation, hypervigilance, aggression, developmental delays, health problems
The developing brain cannot distinguish between types of threat — a frightening parent is as threatening as a predator
Children need adults who are safe and predictable to turn off the stress response

📖  LESSON CONTENT

Children may also minimise or defend the perpetrating parent — this is a survival strategy

LESSON 3.10: BRAIN DEVELOPMENT BY AGE — WHAT TO EXPECT WHEN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can identify age-appropriate developmental expectations for each of their children.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Understanding what is developmentally normal at each age prevents misinterpretation of children&apos;s behaviour
0-2 years: entirely dependent, no impulse control, communication through behaviour, attachment forming
3-5 years: beginning language, magical thinking, limited emotional regulation, egocentric thinking, identity forming
6-11 years: logical thinking developing, peer relationships important, need for mastery and competence
12-18 years: identity exploration, peer influence, risk-taking brain, emotional intensity, need for autonomy and connection
Children who have experienced trauma may present at a developmental age younger than their chronological age
Meeting children at their developmental — not chronological — age is key to effective parenting

LESSON 3.11: WHY ROUTINES AND PREDICTABILITY ARE BRAIN FOOD FOR CHILDREN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand the neurological importance of routines and can establish consistent daily routines for their children.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
The developing brain is a pattern-seeking organ — it uses predictability to feel safe
When the environment is predictable, the survival brain can relax and the learning brain can engage
Routines reduce anxiety by removing uncertainty — children know what is coming next
Consistent routines support: emotional regulation, sleep, learning, appetite, behaviour
Children who have experienced chaos and unpredictability are particularly hungry for routine — even if they resist it initially
Routines do not have to be rigid — they need to be predictable
The morning routine, the bedtime routine, and the mealtime routine are the three most important

LESSON 3.12: WHAT YOUR CHILD&apos;S BEHAVIOUR IS TELLING YOU ABOUT THEIR BRAIN
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents can decode their child&apos;s behaviour as communication about their neurological and emotional state.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
All behaviour is communication — especially in children who do not yet have the language for their experience
Challenging behaviour is almost always a sign of an unmet need or an overwhelmed nervous system
Aggression often communicates: I feel unsafe, I am overwhelmed, I do not know how to handle this
Withdrawal communicates: I am shutting down, I am scared, I have learned that connection is dangerous
Clinginess communicates: I am not sure you will come back, I need to check you are there
Regression (acting younger) communicates: this is too much for my developmental stage right now
Asking what is this behaviour telling me? instead of why are they doing this to me? changes everything
✏️  ACTIVITIES
Behaviour decoding activity — match challenging behaviours to likely unmet needs
Discuss your children&apos;s most challenging behaviours and decode them as a group
Practise responding to the need behind the behaviour rather than the behaviour itself
💭  REFLECTION QUESTIONS

How might responding to the need change the behaviour over time?
📋  ASSESSMENT TASK
Select two of your children&apos;s most challenging behaviours and write a behaviour decoding analysis for each — identifying what need it is communicating and how you will respond to the need.
LESSON 3.13: HOW PLAY BUILDS BRAIN CONNECTIONS
🎯  LEARNING OBJECTIVE
Parents understand the neuroscience of play and can incorporate brain-building play into daily life.
📖  LESSON CONTENT
Play is not a luxury — it is the primary vehicle through which children&apos;s brains develop
During play, multiple brain regions are active simultaneously — building connections across the whole brain
Types of play and what they build: physical play (motor, body awareness, risk assessment), pretend play (narrative, empathy, creativity), social play (theory of mind, cooperation, communication), exploratory play (curiosity, problem-solving, confidence)
Child-led play — where the adult follows the child&apos;s lead — is particularly powerful for attachment and brain development
Parent-child play is more than fun — it is co-regulation, connection and brain-building in action
Screen time is not equivalent to active play — it stimulates but does not build the same connections
✏️  ACTIVITIES
